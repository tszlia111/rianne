# Yeast Fermentation Study — Humanized Text

## Introduction

Yeast fermentation is a biological process where yeast cells break down sugars without oxygen to produce energy, releasing carbon dioxide and ethanol as byproducts. This process plays a key role in everyday food production, including baking and brewing, and it also serves as a straightforward model for studying how cells metabolize nutrients. Since carbon dioxide is a direct byproduct of fermentation, measuring how much is produced offers a reliable way to gauge fermentation activity.

The type of sugar available to yeast has a notable impact on how quickly fermentation occurs. Sugars differ in their structure and need to go through different metabolic pathways before they can enter glycolysis. Glucose, a simple monosaccharide, can be transported directly into yeast cells and used for energy right away. Sucrose, on the other hand, is a disaccharide made up of glucose and fructose, so it first needs to be broken down by the enzyme invertase before it can be metabolized (Sugar Metabolism in Yeasts). While this extra step might seem like it would slow things down, previous research has shown that sucrose can actually produce equal or even greater amounts of carbon dioxide compared to glucose under certain conditions.

Supporting this idea, a study published in the Journal of Undergraduate Biology Laboratory Investigations found that sucrose led to greater carbon dioxide production than glucose during yeast fermentation, suggesting that disaccharides may support higher fermentation activity than one might expect. Similarly, a recent study published in Foods (2022) showed that sugar type significantly influences fermentation dynamics, with fermentable sugars producing considerably more carbon dioxide than non-fermentable alternatives. Artificial sweeteners like sucralose-based products are generally considered non-fermentable because they do not provide a usable carbon source for yeast to metabolize.

The purpose of this study was to examine how different types of sugar affect the rate of yeast fermentation. Three sugar treatments were tested: dextrose, sucrose, and the artificial sweetener Splenda. Fermentation activity was estimated by measuring the diameter of a balloon at its widest point as carbon dioxide accumulated over time. The hypothesis was that fermentable sugars would lead to greater balloon expansion than Splenda, with both sucrose and dextrose producing significantly more carbon dioxide than the artificial sweetener.
